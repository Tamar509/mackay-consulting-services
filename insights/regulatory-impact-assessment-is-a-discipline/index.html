Regulatory impact assessment is a discipline, not a document

28 July 2026

By the time most regulatory impact assessments are commissioned, the decision they are supposed to inform has already been taken. The policy is agreed, the instrument is drafted, and the RIA is required because a guideline says one is required. What arrives is a justification exercise with a cost table attached.

That is a waste of a genuinely useful instrument of government. Regulatory impact assessment, used at the right moment, is the only structured opportunity a ministry gets to ask whether a proposed measure will work before it is bound to defend it.

Three questions that have to be asked early

What is the problem, stated without reference to the solution? A surprising number of regulatory proposals describe the intervention rather than the failure. The absence of a licensing regime is not a problem statement; it is a proposed answer. The problem is the harm, the market failure or the unmet obligation that the absence is producing, and it should be capable of being described in a paragraph without naming an instrument.

Who bears the cost of compliance, and can they carry it? Compliance costs fall unevenly. A registration fee and an annual return are trivial for a bank and material for a trader. Where the regulated population is dominated by micro and small enterprises, a uniform obligation is in practice a selective one: the large comply, the small become informal, and the register stops describing the market. This is the analysis that most often changes a draft, and it is the analysis most often skipped.

Which institution administers this, and with what? Every duty created in a bill is a workload assigned to somebody. An application must be received, assessed, decided, communicated, appealed and recorded. If the assessment cannot say which unit does each of those things, with how many staff and on what system, then the instrument is being drafted on the assumption of an administrative capacity that has not been checked.

The options appraisal is not optional

A serious RIA compares the proposal against at least the counterfactual of doing nothing, and against one materially different alternative — guidance rather than regulation, a code of practice, an amendment to an existing instrument, or better enforcement of what is already law. Where only one option appears, the assessment is not an appraisal; it is a preamble.

If the analysis cannot change the instrument, it is not an assessment.

Write the indicators into the instrument

The most practical thing an RIA can do is to specify, in advance, what would count as the measure having worked and where that data will come from. If the answer is a figure that only the regulator will ever hold, then the duty to collect and publish it belongs in the instrument itself. Otherwise the post-implementation review, when it comes, will be conducted on whatever data happens to exist.

This is the point at which drafting, systems design and evaluation stop being separate exercises. The indicator has to be defined by someone who understands the policy, expressed by someone who can draft the reporting duty, and made collectable by whoever specifies the system the regulator will use. Splitting those three roles across three contracts is how reporting duties end up unenforceable.

A modest standard

None of this requires an elaborate econometric apparatus. In most sectors in most of the region, the data will not support one, and pretending otherwise produces false precision. What it requires is that the questions be asked in the right order, early enough to matter, and that the answers be written down in a form a scrutiny committee can interrogate.

Regulatory impact assessment done to that standard is not paperwork. It is the difference between an instrument that changes behaviour and one that sits on the statute book being cited.
